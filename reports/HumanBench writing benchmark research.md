# Nobody Ranks Models on Sounding Human Yet

As of November 2026 no neutral, standing benchmark ranks AI models by how human their writing reads. The idea behind HumanBench is still open, but the name is not. Existing work covers fragments. Writing leaderboards such as EQ-Bench and Surge's Hemingway-bench score quality and treat "slop" as a side metric. One-off studies show the verdict flips with the judge: persona-prompted GPT-4.5 passed as human 73% of the time in chat, yet a five-person panel of heavy LLM users misclassified only 1 of 300 articles. Vendor sites like HumanizerBench rank humanizer tools by how often they slip past detectors, and Pangram's 2026 detector report includes a per-model table in which, by the vendor's own numbers, every frontier model is caught more than 99% of the time. Building a credible benchmark follows a documented lifecycle: an operational definition, natural tasks, matched human baselines, a pilot with difficulty calibration, a private test split, uncertainty reporting, a one-command harness and versioned maintenance. The bar is low, because only 16% of 445 reviewed benchmarks report any statistics. The evidence favours one design. Blind matched pairs judged by screened expert readers serve as the anchor, a private trained discriminator scales it, a frozen detector panel and distributional stylometry act as diagnostics, and a multiplicative quality gate stops typos and degraded prose from winning. "HumanBench" already names two vision benchmarks and a viral quiz, is one letter from the LLM benchmark HumaneBench, and sits next to the evasion-oriented HumanizerBench, so a different or qualified name is safer. My estimate is that a solo developer can ship a credible v1 in roughly 2–4 months part-time, for about $4K–$7K lean or $15K–$25K fuller. Human reference writing and expert judging are the main costs.

## Writing leaderboards score taste, and only one-off studies test humanness

### Quality benchmarks are plentiful and their judges are shaky

Most writing benchmarks ask whether a text is good, not whether a reader would believe a person wrote it. EQ-Bench Creative Writing v3 is the best-known solo-built writing leaderboard. It scores 96 items (32 prompts × 3 iterations) with a Claude Sonnet 4.6 judge, turns rubric scores and pairwise matchups into Glicko-2 ratings, and costs about **$10 per model** to run. Its maintainer lists slop bias and judge self-bias among the biases it does not control ([EQ-Bench GitHub](https://github.com/EQ-bench/creative-writing-bench)). The slop score counts overused LLM phrases and is shown next to the Elo, but it does not change the Elo by default ([eqbench.com](https://eqbench.com/about.html)). Surge's Hemingway-bench had expert writers make **more than 5,000 blind pairwise comparisons** across eight sub-dimensions. None of the eight is human-likeness. The word appears only in a side comment that "Opus 4.5 had the most human-like feel" ([Surge](https://surgehq.ai/blog/hemingway-bench-ai-writing-leaderboard)). Surge also claims EQ-Bench agreed with expert writers as little as 43% of the time. That is a competitor's figure, but the wider evidence points the same way. The best zero-shot judge on LitBench agrees with human story preferences **73%** of the time ([arXiv 2507.00769](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.00769)). Strong LLMs "barely outperform random baselines" on the Writing Quality benchmark ([arXiv 2504.07532](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07532v3)), and zero-shot judges average about 54% on WritingPreferenceBench ([arXiv 2510.14616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14616v2)). Epoch AI rated Lech Mazur's story benchmark "Flawed" because it combined evaluator panels through an undisclosed bridging procedure ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/lech-mazur-writing/review)). A HumanBench that relies on an LLM to judge "humanness" will share this weakness unless humans calibrate the judge.

A second group of benchmarks measures proxies for human-likeness without using the word. NoveltyBench found that all 20 models it tested produce "significantly less diversity than human writers," and that larger models are often less diverse ([arXiv 2504.05228](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05228v4)). The Creativity Index measures how much of a text can be rebuilt from web snippets. Professional authors score about **66.2% higher** than LLMs on it, and RLHF lowers model scores by about 30% ([arXiv 2410.04265](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.04265)). MirrorBench scores how human-like LLM "user proxies" are in conversation and finds systematic gaps against real users ([arXiv 2601.08118](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08118)). The closest existing human-likeness score comes from Nieth et al. Their register-aware framework computes Maximum Mean Discrepancy over 67 Biber lexico-grammatical features. Every model deviates from humans in every register, and which model comes closest depends on the register, not on model size ([arXiv 2605.23651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23651)). The framework uses no human judges and covers only seven open models.

### One-off studies show the judge decides the verdict

Direct "human or AI?" evidence comes from one-off studies rather than leaderboards, and the results depend on who judges. In Jones and Bergen's three-party Turing test, GPT-4.5 with a persona prompt was judged human **73%** of the time, more often than the real humans. Without the persona it scored 36%. GPT-4o managed about 21%, below ELIZA's 23% ([TechXplore/UCSD](https://techxplore.com/news/2026-05-ai-human-real-humans-classic.html)). Lay readers asked to spot AI poetry scored 46.6%, below chance, and rated the AI poems higher. Labelling a poem as AI lowered its ratings on 13 qualities ([Porter & Machery](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11564748)). Expert readers did far better. In Russell, Karpinska and Iyyer's study, a majority vote of five people who use ChatGPT heavily for writing **misclassified 1 of 300 non-fiction articles**, including paraphrased and "humanized" ones. Non-users caught 56.7% of AI texts but also flagged 51.7% of human texts as AI ([ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v2)). Chakrabarty, Ginsburg and Dhillon found that experts preferred human writers over prompted imitations of those writers. Fine-tuning ChatGPT on an author's work reversed the preference and cut detector flags from **97% to 3%**, at a median cost of $81 per author ([arXiv 2510.13939](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13939)). On voice imitation, "Catch Me If You Can?" tested more than 400 everyday authors. Models matched news and email styles but failed on informal blogs and forums ([arXiv 2509.14543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14543v1)). A 2026 follow-up found that every personalization method scored below a cross-author authorship-verification floor. It also found that LLM-judge, authorship-embedding and stylometric metrics barely correlate with one another (|r| < 0.07) ([arXiv 2604.26460](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26460)).

### Vendors already rank humanizers and detectability, from interested positions

Commercial leaderboards exist, but they rank tools rather than models, and the companies running them sell those tools. HumanizerBench is run by the humanizer vendor WriteHuman, which ranks itself first. Each month it scores 16 tools on 33 prompts against five detectors, using a composite weighted 42% detector bypass, 32% meaning preservation, 16% readability and 10% consistency ([humanizerbench.com](https://humanizerbench.com)). humanizereval.com keeps its data under the StealthGPT-Labs GitHub organisation, ranks StealthGPT first, and reports quality without letting it affect rank ([humanizereval](https://www.humanizereval.com/)). A September 2026 Vulsar AI benchmark pitted 24 LLMs against human writers, but it scores predicted reader preference, not detectability ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/new-benchmark-pits-human-writers-against-24-llms-across-475-prompts/)). For models, Pangram's July 2026 technical report lists per-model miss rates for 26 generators. Pangram 4 misses less than 0.7% of output from every frontier model, from Claude Haiku 4.5 (0.13%) to Grok 4.3 (0.605%) ([arXiv 2607.27183](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27183)). That works as a detectability leaderboard, but it is vendor-run and already saturated. The academic work closest to an indistinguishability leaderboard is MIT's Generalized Turing Test. It ranks nine LLMs by "Turing Scores" from imitation games in which models imitate other models, not humans ([arXiv 2605.10851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10851)).

| Project | What it scores | How human-likeness appears | Why it is not HumanBench |
|---|---|---|---|
| EQ-Bench Creative Writing v3 / Longform | LLM-judged creative quality (Elo, rubric) | Slop score beside the Elo | Quality first; slop is a phrase list; LLM judge |
| Hemingway-bench (Surge) | Expert pairwise quality, 8 sub-dimensions | Anecdotal remark only | No humanness score |
| LitBench, WQ, WritingPreferenceBench | Human preference between texts | None | Preference is not indistinguishability |
| NoveltyBench, Creativity Index | Diversity or novelty against human text | Indirect proxy | One axis only |
| Register-aware MMD (Nieth et al. 2026) | Biber-feature distance to human text | Explicit, linguistic only | No human judges; open models only |
| Jones & Bergen Turing tests | Interrogator verdicts in live chat | Explicit | One-off; chat only |
| Russell et al. 2025 | Expert, lay and detector accuracy | Explicit | Evaluates detectors on fixed texts |
| Chakrabarty et al. 2025 | Expert and lay preference plus detector flags | Explicit | One-off; literary imitation |
| Catch Me If You Can (2025) | Imitation of 400+ everyday authors | Authorship and detector ensemble | Voice only; no leaderboard |
| HumanizerBench, humanizereval | Detector bypass by humanizer tools | Explicit, framed as evasion | Vendor-run; ranks tools, not models |
| Pangram 4 report | Per-model detector miss rates | Implicit | One vendor detector; saturated |
| Generalized Turing Test (MIT 2026) | Model-vs-model imitation | Explicit | Target is other models, not humans |

The gap is specific. Nobody runs a standing, refreshable, model-level leaderboard whose headline is human-likeness, judged against matched human writing across everyday registers, calibrated to expert readers, and gated on quality. Demand is visible. Merriam-Webster made "slop" its 2025 Word of the Year ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/word-of-the-year)). In November 2025 OpenAI announced that ChatGPT would finally follow instructions to avoid em dashes ([TechCrunch](https://www.techcrunch.com/2025/11/14/openai-says-its-fixed-chatgpts-em-dash-problem/)). Undetectable AI reported 11 million users within 18 months of launch ([PR Newswire](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/undetectable-ai-reaches-11-million-users-in-18-months-since-launch-302303963.html)). Labs compete on sounding less machine-made, and they have no neutral yardstick. Two caveats apply. The searches covered X/Twitter poorly, and a lab or detector vendor could run a private eval of this kind with no public page.

## Benchmark building follows a mapped lifecycle that most builders cut short

Two recent reviews have turned benchmark-building folk wisdom into checklists. BetterBench defines **46 best practices** across design, implementation, documentation, maintenance and retirement. Applied to 24 well-known benchmarks, it found that 14 reported no uncertainty or significance and 17 had no easy replication script. Implementation was the weakest stage, with a mean score of 6.2 against 10.8 for design ([arXiv 2411.12990](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12990)). Bean et al. reviewed **445 LLM benchmarks**. Only 16.0% used uncertainty estimates or statistical tests, and only 32.4% compared against a human baseline. Of the benchmarks that defined what they measured, 47.8% used a contested definition. Their 28-item construct-validity checklist opens with "define the phenomenon" and "measure the phenomenon and only the phenomenon" ([arXiv 2511.04703](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04703v1)). "Human-like writing" is the kind of abstract, contested construct that review warns about, so its checklist is a good backbone for HumanBench.

The stages come in a fairly fixed order. The first is purpose and an operational definition: who will use the score and for what decision, which sub-components count, what is excluded, and how the score could be gamed. Task design comes next. Ofir Press, a co-creator of SWE-bench, argues for "natural questions that some category of humans ask on a frequent basis." Each task should specify a request, constraints, stopping criteria and a scorer ([Ofir Press](https://ofir.io/How-to-Build-Good-Language-Modeling-Benchmarks/)). Items should be built with domain experts, and every item checked. Model-generated items take minutes rather than months, but they need human verification and inherit model biases ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/evaluating-ai-systems)). A pilot then calibrates difficulty, and Aider's Polyglot benchmark is the clearest solo example. Aider ran 697 Exercism problems past seven top models, dropped the ones all seven solved, and kept 225 so that frontier models would spread across roughly 5–50%. It launched with o1 at 61.7% ([Aider](https://aider.chat/2024/12/21/polyglot.html)). Press notes that a benchmark mostly solved at launch attracts little work, and that shelf life is often about a year.

Size guidance conflicts, and the right size depends on the scoring design. Jason Wei recommends at least 1,000 examples ([Jason Wei](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/evals)). Press suggests 300–500 tasks and calls anything under about 150 too noisy. Evan Miller's power analysis puts the figure at **969 independent questions** to detect a 3-point difference with 80% power ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)). EQ-Bench manages with 96 items because each item feeds many pairwise comparisons. Miller's paper also covers the statistics most benchmarks skip. Report standard errors. Cluster them by prompt when several samples share one, because in real data clustered errors came out up to three times larger. Sample each prompt several times to reduce variance, and compare models with paired differences. His advice on temperature is blunt: "don't change the sampling temperature to reduce variance."

Defences against contamination and gaming belong before release, not after. The standard kit has three parts. The first is a private held-out split; SimpleBench, for example, keeps its questions "fully private" ([SimpleBench](https://simple-bench.com/about.html)). The second is a canary string in every released file, as BIG-bench did. The third is periodic fresh items, which LiveBench adds monthly ([arXiv 2406.19314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19314)). Leaderboard policy matters as well. "The Leaderboard Illusion" documented Meta privately testing 27 variants on Chatbot Arena before Llama 4. It estimated that Google and OpenAI received 19.2% and 20.4% of all Arena data, against 29.7% for 83 open-weight models combined ([arXiv 2504.20879](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879)). That is an argument for publishing every submitted score and treating all models alike.

How a benchmark is released and maintained decides whether people adopt it. BIG-bench lost traction partly because it was hard to install and run, and Anthropic dropped it ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/evaluating-ai-systems)). A solo builder needs no custom stack. UK AISI's Inspect supports more than 20 providers, model-graded scorers and a log viewer ([Inspect](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/)), and EQ-Bench runs as a plain Python script against OpenRouter-compatible endpoints. The rest of the package is HELM's norm of releasing all raw prompts and completions ([arXiv 2211.09110](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.09110)), a datasheet ([Gebru et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010)), and Croissant metadata. The NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations & Datasets track requires Croissant metadata with responsible-AI fields ([NeurIPS 2026](https://nips.cc/Conferences/2026/EvaluationsDatasetsHosting)). Wei's criteria for adoption are a single headline number, easy running, meaningful tasks, correct grading, slow saturation and active promotion, including offering to run the eval for others. Maintenance means treating the judge as part of the versioned benchmark. EQ-Bench's judge has changed over time, and Epoch's "Flawed" rating of Lech Mazur's benchmark came from an undisclosed bridge between judge panels.

## Expert readers on matched pairs should anchor the score

### Aim at a skilled human writing the same brief

Defining the construct is the most consequential choice. The evidence supports this definition: a text is human-like to the degree that a discerning reader cannot tell it apart from a competent human text written to the same brief in the same register, with task quality at least at the human level. The definition should explicitly exclude factual accuracy, helpfulness and "warmth." The last exclusion matters because HumT DumT found that a human-like tone goes with warmth, social closeness and low status, traits linked to deception and over-reliance, and that users often preferred less human-like outputs ([arXiv 2502.13259](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13259)). A benchmark that rewarded chatty anthropomorphism would push labs toward a style they are trying to remove.

Everyday writing should come first among the registers, because it is the least studied and the most useful: work emails, Slack or text messages, product reviews, forum comments, cover letters and support replies, plus personal essays and short fiction. Russell et al. limit their expert-detection claims to non-fiction articles. Imitation fails most on informal registers, and model rankings change from one register to another ([arXiv 2605.23651](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23651)). Each register should have fixed length bands. Texts under 50 words should be kept off the detector axis: Pangram does not score them ([Pangram blog](https://www.pangram.com/blog/third-party-pangram-evals)), and in Pangram's re-run of the UChicago data GPTZero caught none of the short AI texts at a 1% false-positive rate ([arXiv 2607.27183](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27183)).

The benchmark needs separate tracks, because how a model is prompted or adapted changes its detectability more than which model it is. A persona prompt doubled GPT-4.5's pass rate from 36% to 73%. On Epoch AI's style-imitation set, Pangram 4 missed 2.86% of texts, about eight times its 0.34% miss rate on ordinary prompts ([arXiv 2607.27183](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27183)). Fine-tuning cut detector flags from 97% to 3%. Base models continuing human text look 96.7–98.8% human to GPTZero and Pangram, against 17–30% for their instruction-tuned versions ([arXiv 2605.19516](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19516v1)). Three tracks cover what model providers control:

| Track | Setup | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Default | No style instruction | What users actually get |
| Instructed | One fixed, published "write naturally" or persona prompt | |
| Voice | Few-shot imitation of a consenting author | |
| Adapted | Fine-tuned or humanizer-processed entries | Optional, accepted only if clearly labelled |

The Default track should carry the headline, because default output is what people mean when they complain that AI writing is easy to spot.

### Only screened expert readers still separate frontier models

The headline measurement should be a blind two-alternative forced choice. The judge sees the model's text and a matched human text for the same brief, in random order, and picks the one a human wrote. The model's pass rate is the share of pairs in which the judge picks the model's text, so 50% means the two cannot be told apart. The format solves three problems at once. It sidesteps **label bias**, since readers' ratings drop when they know a text is AI-written (Porter & Machery) and here the judge only has to pick. It is more **consistent**, because pairwise judgments beat absolute ratings: RankME showed relative assessment outperforms direct scoring ([arXiv 1803.05928](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.05928v1.pdf)), and a Likert-scale NLG study reported only a "moderate" ICC of 0.45 ([arXiv 1707.06875](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.06875)). And it makes **bad writing useless** as a strategy, because the human reference is competent. The design is my inference from those sources, and the pilot needs to validate it.

The judges must be experts. Russell et al.'s experts individually averaged a 92.7% true-positive rate with 4.0% false positives, and as a majority vote they were near perfect. Yet one expert caught only 16.7% of o1-Pro articles, so panels of three to five with majority voting are necessary ([arXiv 2501.15654](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v2)). Karpinska et al. found that Mechanical Turk workers could not tell model stories from human ones even with strict qualification filters, while English teachers could ([EMNLP 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.97)). Clark et al. found that brief training raised lay accuracy to at most 55% ([arXiv 2107.00061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00061)). Russell's experts were heavy LLM users, not professional editors, which suggests a cheaper way to recruit them: screen a crowd panel for heavy LLM use, then keep only those who pass a qualification set of items whose authorship is known. The benchmark should also report a lay-reader pass rate as a secondary "general audience" number, since the two groups can disagree completely.

Experts cannot judge every pair for every model at every release, so a machine judge has to extend their verdicts, and the evidence favours a trained discriminator over a prompted LLM. Zero-shot GPT-4o caught 71.3% of AI articles with a 6.7% false-positive rate, and o1 caught only 42.2%. An expert-written guidebook of tells raised GPT-4o only to 78.0% ([arXiv 2501.15654](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15654v2)). LLM judges reading Turing-test transcripts scored below chance ([arXiv 2407.08853](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08853v1)), and judges favour their own outputs more the better they recognise them ([arXiv 2404.13076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). Classifiers built on fine-tuned embeddings, by contrast, tell which of five model families wrote a text with **97.1%** accuracy ([arXiv 2502.12150](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12150v2)).

The scalable judge should therefore be a private discriminator, trained per register on held-out human and model text. It should be retrained every version, never released, and checked against the expert panel on a stratified subset at each release. A panel of LLM judges still has two uses: the quality gate, and written rationales. The panel should come from two or three model families. No judge should score its own family's outputs, every pair should run in both orders, and length should be controlled with a regression like AlpacaEval's, which raised correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98 ([arXiv 2404.04475](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475)). The pilot should also test whether LLM judges do better on side-by-side pairs than on single texts, since no source answers that.

### Detectors belong on the board as frozen, continuous diagnostics

Detectors are cheap and reproducible, but four findings rule them out as the headline. They are **saturated on default output**: Pangram 4 misses under 0.7% of every frontier model's text, and the independent UChicago study found Pangram's false-negative rate never exceeded 2% for any of four generators across six genres ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/pangram-achieves-near-perfect-results-in-ai-text-detection-tests-study-reveals/)). They **drift between versions**: Gemini 3.1 Pro's miss rate fell from 6.38% to 0.555% between two Pangram releases, which reshuffled the rankings ([arXiv 2607.27183](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27183)). They **track post-training style rather than humanness**, as base models fooling them shows, and perplexity-based detectors can even misfire in the wrong direction because modern models write with lower perplexity than humans in ways the detectors do not expect ([arXiv 2603.17522](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17522)). And they are **easy to optimize against**. Reinforcement learning with a detector reward cut detector AUROC from 0.84 to 0.63 ([ICLR 2024](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/1f9f07df0992ce21698d800eaa891bd8-Abstract-Conference.html)), AuthorMist reached 78.6–96.2% evasion ([arXiv 2503.08716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08716v1)), and an ELOQUENT 2026 entry evaded a surrogate detector 98.5% of the time ([arXiv 2609.22221](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.22221)).

Used carefully, detectors still add signal. Score each model by the AUROC with which each detector separates its outputs from the matched human texts, because continuous scores spread models out even when binary miss rates all sit below 1%. Freeze a varied panel for each version. Pangram anchors it with the strongest independent record, though that record rests on three studies and the first author of one later co-wrote Pangram's technical report. GPTZero or Originality.ai adds a second commercial view. Binoculars or Fast-DetectGPT with a pinned scorer model gives reproducibility. An interpretable classifier on tell features belongs too, since an XGBoost model of this kind matched transformer detectors in 2026 benchmarking ([arXiv 2603.17522](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17522)), and ideally the team adds a private detector of its own. Leave out watermark and retrieval detectors: they test where a text came from rather than how it reads, and Gemini's SynthID watermark would confound the comparison.

Detector scores also need bookkeeping. Record detector versions and query dates, and re-score everything whenever a detector updates. Publish each detector's false-positive rate on the benchmark's own human references, split by register, length, and native versus ESL writers, because the Stanford TOEFL study found seven detectors averaged a **61.22%** false-positive rate on non-native essays ([arXiv 2304.02819](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.02819)). Check terms of service as well. Pangram's forbid users to "circumvent any feature of the Service" ([Pangram ToS](https://www.pangram.com/terms-of-service)), so use official APIs within their limits and disclose the research purpose, or partner with the vendor.

### Distributional stylometry exposes sameness single texts hide

The cheapest open sub-scores compare the distribution of a model's output with a matched human distribution in the same register. The building blocks are already measured. In grammar, models use nominalizations at **1.5–2 times** the human rate ([Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)). In vocabulary, excess style words mark at least 13.5% of 2024 PubMed abstracts ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016v5)), and some slop patterns appear over 1,000 times more often in model text than in human text ([Antislop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061)). Punctuation varies widely, with em-dash rates from 0.0 to 9.1 per 1,000 words across 12 models ([arXiv 2603.27006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27006)). Stories from different models also resemble each other more than human stories do ([arXiv 2606.17350](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17350)). That sameness across samples is probably the hardest tell to fake at the level of a model, since a model can write one convincing text and still write nearly the same text a hundred times.

Two cautions shape how these metrics enter the score. First, word lists go stale and get optimized away. The Antislop sampler suppresses more than 8,000 patterns and cuts slop by 90% with no loss on standard benchmarks, and Wikipedia's field guide now lists em-dash overuse as a historical sign ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Distributional distances hold up better: MMD over Biber features, over-representation ratios, and compression and template rates ([arXiv 2509.19163](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19163v1)). Any tell lists should be refreshed every version. Second, these metrics disagree with one another, so they should be reported as a profile and kept out of the headline.

### A multiplicative quality gate closes the typo loophole

A pure "pass as human" score invites deliberate degradation. Turing-test interrogators cited typos as evidence of a human, and some judged a witness human because it made an error ([arXiv 2503.23674](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23674)). RAID's standard evasion attacks include misspellings, homoglyphs and zero-width spaces ([RAID](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.07940)). Pangram's audit of 19 humanizers found that all of them tend to reduce quality: even the best group was judged more fluent than the original only 26% of the time ([arXiv 2501.03437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03437)). The fix is a gate. It multiplies the pass rate by an indicator that the task was done, the meaning preserved, and fluency at least at a low percentile of the human references. It also penalizes surface noise (typos, odd casing, unusual characters) above the human rate for that register. HumanizerBench subtracts penalties for meaning drift and length inflation. humanizereval reports quality without letting it affect rank, which is exactly what to avoid. LLM judges are poor at fine-grained taste, so the gate should be a minimum standard validated against expert labels, not a quality ranking.

| Signal | Question it answers | Role in HumanBench | Main risk and guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expert pairwise panel | Can a discerning reader pick out the human text? | Anchor; sets the headline on a calibration subset each release | Individual experts vary; majority of 3–5, known-answer checks, lay pool reported separately |
| Private trained discriminator | The same question, for every model and prompt | Extends the headline to all models, calibrated to experts | Overfitting; retrain each version, never release |
| Frozen detector panel | How well do detectors separate outputs from matched human text (AUROC)? | Diagnostic sub-score | Saturation, drift, gaming; continuous scores, pinned versions, own false-positive audit |
| Distributional stylometry | How far is the output distribution from human text in this register? | Diagnostic sub-score | Stale word lists; use distributions, refresh tells |
| Diversity across samples | Does the model keep writing the same text? | Model-level diagnostic | Prompt effects; matched prompts, several samples |
| Quality and fidelity gate | Was the task done at human-level competence? | Multiplier on the headline | Weak quality judges; minimum standard validated by experts |

## Clean human text and a private split decide whether scores last

### Post-2022 "human" writing is already contaminated

Every human-likeness score depends on the human reference, and text written after ChatGPT's release is no longer reliably human. An EPFL study estimated that **33–46%** of Mechanical Turk workers used LLMs on a summarization task ([arXiv 2306.07899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.07899)). A follow-up found that about 30% did so without being told anything, and that asking them not to only halved the rate ([arXiv 2310.15683](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.15683)). By late 2024 about 18% of US consumer financial complaints and up to 24% of corporate press releases were LLM-assisted ([arXiv 2502.09747](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.09747)). In a 65,000-URL sample, AI-generated articles made up 51.7% of new web articles in May 2025 ([Axios](https://axios.com/2025/10/14/ai-generated-writing-humans)). Human writing is also drifting toward model style. A 2026 preprint found excess LLM-associated vocabulary in 89% of open-access PubMed Central papers by the end of 2025 ([arXiv 2608.10715](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10715)). Human baselines therefore need a date stamp, and HumanBench should state which era of human writing it uses as its target.

### Build the reference set in two tiers

The reference set should come in two tiers. The archival tier draws on pre-November-2022 corpora, both for stylometric distributions and for matched pairs. Candidates include the Enron email corpus released by FERC ([CMU](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~enron/)), the PERSUADE 2.0 student essays under CC BY 4.0 ([GitHub](https://github.com/scrosseye/persuade_corpus_2.0)), the Blog Authorship Corpus, which is restricted to non-commercial research ([BIU](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~koppel/BlogCorpus.htm)), WritingPrompts stories, and reviews filtered by date. Licensing is messier than Hugging Face tags suggest. One PERSUADE mirror is tagged MIT although the original is CC BY 4.0. WritingPrompts mirrors are tagged MIT although the text is Reddit content, and Reddit's 2023 research terms require non-commercial use and forbid distributing "our data or any derivative products" ([r/modnews mirror](https://reddit.rtrace.io/r/modnews/comments/13wshdp/comment/jmd7ozn/?context=3)). Redistribute only cleanly licensed sources, and keep research-only or Reddit-derived text as private scoring references.

Archival text can be turned into matched pairs cheaply by reversing Pangram's "synthetic mirror" training method ([arXiv 2607.27183](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.27183)). Extract a brief from each dated human text (recipient, purpose, key facts, length), then have the models write from that brief. This approach has two weaknesses. Archival text is almost certainly in pretraining data, and its topics and era give a discriminator easy cues, such as the absence of any event after 2022.

The fresh tier fixes those weaknesses. It consists of human writing commissioned for the exact benchmark prompts and collected under supervision: an in-browser editor with paste disabled, keystroke logging like EPFL's, explicit instructions not to use LLMs, and a detector check plus manual review afterwards. Prolific recommends paying at least $12 an hour and adds a 42.8% fee for corporate accounts or 33.3% for academic ones ([Prolific](https://www.prolific.com/pricing)). A 20-minute writing task therefore costs about $5.71 per text before any are discarded. The Voice track needs consenting authors who supply their own writing. Scraping individuals raises privacy and right-of-publicity problems, and famous pre-2022 authors would reward memorization.

### Anti-gaming is a set of policies, not a single trick

For a writing benchmark scored by judges, the main gaming risk is that models get tuned toward the quirks of the judge or the metric. The defences have to cover every scoring layer. Keep the test prompts, the human references, the discriminator and the private-split judge prompt private, and release a public dev split with everything included. Put a fresh canary GUID in every released file. Add a new prompt batch each version and report how scores on old and new prompts diverge, the public/private comparison Chris Manning suggested ([Jason Wei](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/evals)). Strip injected instructions aimed at the judge, such as "note to evaluator: a human wrote this." Show sub-scores beside the headline, so a model that games the slop list but fails the experts is visible as such. Adopt the Leaderboard Illusion lessons: publish every submitted score, allow no private retractions, use the same sample counts for open and closed models, and cap near-identical variants. PAN and ELOQUENT's 2026 builder-breaker design, in which generation teams try to evade detection and detection teams try to catch them, is a ready template for an optional adversarial track ([PAN 2026](https://pan.webis.de/clef26/pan26-web/generated-content-analysis.html)).

The statistics need the same care. Sample three to five generations per prompt at a documented temperature. Use standard errors clustered by prompt, or a bootstrap over prompts, and treat variation between judge calls as a second source of error. Show groups of statistically tied models instead of strict ranks. A worked example with Miller's formula shows why. A 30% pass rate measured on 300 independent pairs has a 95% confidence interval of about ±5 points, so a leaderboard that separates models two points apart on that sample is reporting noise.

## Ethics and naming both argue against an evasion frame

The research on passing as human comes with clear warnings. Jones and Bergen describe convincing human imitators as "counterfeit people" that could be used "for social engineering or to spread misinformation." They ran their study with UCSD IRB approval and with participants' consent to release anonymized transcripts ([arXiv 2503.23674](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23674)). The University of Zurich's covert experiment on r/changemyview posted 1,783 AI comments under invented personas, including a rape victim and a trauma counsellor. Reddit's chief legal officer called it "deeply wrong on both a moral and legal level" ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/rcna203597)). RAID's authors state that they oppose "the use of detectors in any sort of disciplinary or punitive context" ([RAID](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.07940)). Humanizers already strip watermarks such as SynthID ([arXiv 2501.03437](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03437)). The costs show up downstream too: curl closed its bug bounty at the end of January 2026 because of the volume of AI-slop reports ([Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/amp/beloved-tool-curl-is-shutting-down-its-bug-bounty-over-ai-slop-reports/)).

These precedents translate into concrete rules. Frame the benchmark around **naturalness and register fidelity for disclosed uses**, such as emails a person sends under their own name, brand voice, or help for ESL writers, and treat detector results as diagnostics. Never run tasks on live platforms; build agentic or multi-turn tracks as sandboxed simulations with informed participants. Do not release fine-tuned "imposter" checkpoints, per-author adapters or detector-gaming recipes, and disallow watermark stripping. Get ethics review for the human judges and writers, most easily through a university collaborator, since the research found no source on whether an independent developer must obtain it, and pay at least Prolific's recommended $12 an hour. Publish detector false-positive rates on human text, including ESL and AI-polished human writing, so the benchmark helps calibrate detectors instead of legitimizing them as judges of who is human. The extra help this gives bad actors is probably small next to the existing humanizer industry, but a leaderboard does put optimization pressure on frontier labs, which is why the quality gate and the framing matter.

A GitHub name search returns 264 repositories, many of them tied to the humanbenchmark.com reaction-time site ([GitHub search](https://github.com/search?q=humanbench&type=repositories)). ## A solo builder can ship a credible v1 in two to four months

The schedule and budget below are my estimates. They combine sourced unit prices with assumptions about scope. For judging, EQ-Bench's roughly $10 per model, scaled to three times the items and two or three judges, gives about $30–$100 per model. Human work uses Prolific's $12 an hour plus a 33–43% fee. Detection uses Pangram's measured cost of about $0.023 per correctly flagged passage in the UChicago study ([Pangram blog](https://www.pangram.com/blog/third-party-pangram-evals)), and batch APIs are reported at about 50% off by secondary sources ([Enterprise DNA](https://enterprisedna.co/resources/guides/guide-anthropic-batch-api)). The $30–$60 hourly rate for expert readers is an assumption, not a sourced figure. Current token prices were not checked and should be recomputed from live pricing pages before committing.

| Phase | Part-time duration | Main outputs | Rough cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Purpose, construct, threat model, ethics, name | 1 week | Design doc with operational definition, pre-registered headline and aggregation, threat model, ethics plan, name and domain checks | ~$0 |
| 1. Tasks and prompts | 1–2 weeks | 6–8 registers, length bands, 300–500 briefs split into public dev and private test sets; fixed Instructed-track prompt | ~$0 |
| 2. Human references | 2–6 weeks (the bottleneck) | Archival tier with derived briefs; 300–2,000 commissioned, supervised texts; datasheet | $1.7K–$11.4K |
| 3. Harness | ~1 week, overlapping | One-command runner (Inspect or an OpenRouter script), caching, pinned model and detector IDs, logged decoding settings | ~$0 |
| 4. Pilot | 1–2 weeks | 20–50 prompts × 5–8 models; small expert pairwise study; judge–human and inter-rater agreement; Aider-style filtering | $200–$800 |
| 5. Full runs | ~1 week | 20–50 models × 3 samples; detector panel; stylometry; discriminator; LLM-judge quality gate | $1K–$6K |
| 6. Expert validation | 1–3 weeks | 300–600 stratified pairs × 3–5 screened experts; agreement statistics; calibration of the machine judge | $0.5K–$3.6K |
| 7. Release | 2–3 weeks | arXiv report with error analysis; Hugging Face dev split with Croissant metadata; static, dated leaderboard with tied groups; canary; licence | ~$0 |
| Maintenance | Hours per new model; quarterly refresh | Add new frontier models within days; fresh prompt batch and recalibration each version | ~$30–$150 per model |

Adding up the rows gives about **$4K–$7K** for a lean v1 and **$15K–$25K** for a fuller one. The lean version has 20 models, 300 commissioned texts focused on registers where archival data is thin (Slack, DMs, cover letters), and a screened crowd of heavy LLM users as the expert pool. The fuller version has 50 models, 2,000 commissioned texts, professional editors as experts, and a separate lay-reader study. Professional writers in place of Prolific contributors would multiply the commissioning line several times over. On a tight budget, cut models, registers and commissioned volume. Do not cut the matched human baseline, the private split, the confidence intervals, the quality gate, or one expert validation study per version, because without them the leaderboard is not credible. Time runs to roughly 2–4 months part-time, and the human-reference phase dominates it.

The launch should follow the playbook that worked for solo projects. Launch with 20–50 models, covering every frontier closed model and the top open-weight ones, all with confidence intervals. Post the technical report on arXiv first, then submit to the NeurIPS Evaluations & Datasets track or to ACL, EMNLP or COLM. Add new frontier models within days of their release, since speed is much of a solo leaderboard's value. Offer to run the benchmark for labs and open-model fine-tuners, and get it listed by aggregators such as Epoch's hub, which favours benchmarks that are economically relevant, unsaturated and widely used ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/about)). Publish a named contact, a feedback channel and a retirement statement, as BetterBench recommends. Plan v2 from the start. If the Default track saturates, meaning models reach 50% against experts, the next round needs harder discriminators: longer texts, multi-turn threads, more demanding registers and other languages.

## Conclusion

The missing piece is a standardized judge, not another metric. Every ingredient HumanBench needs already exists in someone's paper: matched human text, detector AUROC, Biber distances, slop profiles, diversity scores and expert panels. The same kind of output is "more human than humans" to one audience and caught 299 times out of 300 by another. That spread shows the field's real measurement problem is who reads the text. A benchmark that fixes, publishes and periodically re-checks an expert-calibrated judge, and ties every cheap signal back to it, would give the field a reference point it does not have. The same logic says HumanBench will mostly measure post-training style rather than raw capability. Base models already look human to detectors, and author-level fine-tuning erases the tells that experts rely on. A model's distance from human writing is therefore largely a choice its maker makes during post-training. That is why labs advertising fixes for em dashes and bullet-heavy replies are the natural first users, and why a version of the benchmark framed around evasion would end up serving humanizer vendors instead.

The target also moves at both ends. Models will get better at sounding human, and human writing keeps drifting toward model style, as the PubMed vocabulary data shows. So "human-like" can only mean "like dated human writing from a stated era and register," and the benchmark has a built-in expiry date unless its references and discriminators are refreshed on a schedule. Treating that refresh cycle as part of the design, rather than as later maintenance, is what would separate a lasting HumanBench from the one-off studies it would replace.
